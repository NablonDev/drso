The problems, in order of money lost

1. The lattice cannot draw a straight line at most angles

93 angle points · 103 tension towers · heading step 7.1° at 400 m

Sites are 50 m apart and a span is at most 400 m, so the search can only head in directions whose tangent is a multiple of 50/400. A line at 3° is drawn as alternating 0° and 7.1° spans, and every alternation is priced as a B1 tower (₹0.69 Cr instead of ₹0.48 Cr). The search therefore believes any route not aligned with the bee-line costs ₹0.2 Cr per tower more than it does, and prefers routes that hug the grid directions. The heading_slack option that priced small bends as straight was switched off on 6 October because it over-corrected by ₹27–32 Cr: the slack let real small bends go free too.

String-pulling repairs some of this afterwards (₹4.8 Cr of straighten moves on this solve), but it can only straighten what the search already chose. Routes the search never ranked first because of phantom bends are never seen.

2. The search prices one thing and the spotting builds another

₹567 Cr searched → ₹628 Cr built

Three sources of drift. Steering: the 20 % near-line discount and the hill surcharge are in the search price. Towers: site costs use the suspension tower's foundation and base area for every type, and a forced crossing type is approximated. Terrain: the search prices one +18 m extension where a span fails clearance; the spotting then raises 112 towers and adds towers on ridges. The drift is not the problem by itself. The problem is that it is not uniform, so two routes ₹5 Cr apart in the search can swap places once built, and nothing feeds the built price back into the search.

3. Two stages, repaired locally

14.7 min of spotting · 8 violations left · 50 relaxed crossings

Rules are rasterised at 20 m for the search and checked exactly in spotting. Where the two disagree the spotting nudges, slides, splits turns and re-spots crossings. On this solve the crossing refinement alone ran seven minutes and still reported "no legal layout within the refinement reach" at five power-line crossings. Every repair is local and greedy: it fixes one tower given its neighbours, and stops at the first legal cheaper candidate. The 45 straightening, merge and vertex moves that followed are hill-climbing on top of that.

4. The forest cap is a price search, not a constrained search

6 passes · 300 s · landed at 2.0 ha of 5

Bisection on a multiplier finds the smallest multiplier whose grid line is under the cap minus a 1 ha margin. That is a Lagrangian relaxation with one multiplier for the whole route: the result is feasible but not the cheapest feasible route, and when the forest polygons are lumpy the route jumps from well over the cap to well under it, as here. Six GPU passes are spent to find a number that a second resource in the state could carry in one.

5. Time is spent where it does not change the answer

compile 167 s with a warm cache · refine 222 s on 318 towers, 318 kept · spotting single-threaded

The refinement re-searches a 600 m corridor along the whole route, but the 50 m and 25 m answers had the same tower count and most of the route is straight runs where 25 m changes nothing. The compile rebuilds the rule rasters per solve even when nothing about the rules or layers changed. Spotting is pure Python and Shapely on one core while the GPU idles, and the previous job died of a CUDA out-of-memory in the refinement chunking before the fix in 040be30.

6. Structural limits of the frame

no backward detours · ±60° headings · band chosen by hand · height outside the search

The frame rotated along the bee-line is what makes the exact search cheap, and it is also what rules out a U-turn round a block wider than the band, a leg that runs backwards to reach a crossing at 90°, and any route outside the band the drafter drew. Tower height is chosen after the route, so the search cannot trade a taller tower for a shorter detour. These are the right calls for v1 and they are limits, not bugs.

Fixes on the effort × gain grid

Gain is in rupees on the reported route, in minutes per solve, or in violations removed. Effort is engineering days in this codebase.

Do now · small effort, high gain

  1. Straight-run primitives: multi-span collinear edges in the lattice so fine headings are priced as A towers (fix 1).
  2. One backward pass of the same search: gives the forest-cap gap closure and the alternative routes from one extra 50 s pass (fix 10).
  3. One re-priced search pass with the spotted route's true prices fed back (fix 2).
  4. Refine only round bends and crossings, not the whole route (fix 3).
  5. Spot crossings in parallel processes (fix 4).
  6. Coarse 100 m pass first on wide bands, then the 50 m search in its corridor (fix 11).

Do next · medium effort, high gain

  1. Leg-graph vertex siting: straight legs priced by the exact tower programme, replacing string-pulling and the local moves (fix 12).
  2. Clearance height per edge on the GPU, priced into the span before the search (fix 5).
  3. Forest hectares as a second resource if the backward pass leaves a gap (fix 6).
  4. Cache the compiled rule set keyed by rules, costs and layer versions (fix 7).
  5. Checker rules into spotting (fix 8) and a local lattice at each crossing (fix 9).

Later · large effort, high gain

  1. Non-monotone detector: a coarse turn-aware search; only where it back-tracks, segment the band and re-run the monotone search piecewise.
  2. Tower height in the state: type × extension, so clearance and detour trade in the search exactly.
  3. Cost-versus-hectares frontier for the client instead of one cap (bi-objective labels).

Not worth it now

  1. Finer global lattice (25 m everywhere): memory ×4 and time ×8 for the same phantom-bend problem.
  2. Turning heading_slack back on: it under-prices real bends (measured ₹27–32 Cr).
  3. A metaheuristic (GA, annealing) on top: slower than the exact search and no guarantee.
1

Straight-run primitives in the lattice

gain: fewer phantom bends, ₹5–15 Cr on a 100 km routeeffort: 2–3 dayssolver.make_grid · transition_matrix · _edge_geom

The lattice already uses every span vector in the 100–400 m annulus, which is what the ETH/Gilytics optimiser does too, so any heading a single span can draw continues straight at no bend price. The gap is the headings a single span cannot draw: between 0° and 7.1° there is nothing. Add edges that stand for two or three collinear spans, for example (16, 1) sites = two 400 m spans at 3.6°, or (24, 1) = three spans at 2.4°. The intermediate tower is an A tower whose site must be legal, and the edge's strip cost is sampled along the whole run. Headings then come in steps of 2.4° wherever the ground is straight enough, and the DP prices such a run as suspension towers, which is what the spotting would build. This is the state-lattice "closed under continue-straight" property from robot path planning; the DP stays exact.

  • Keep the bend price between a primitive and its neighbours on the true angle difference.
  • Forbid primitives across crossings so the crossing rules keep their single-span checks.
  • Measure on BHU-SHI-0224: angle points should drop well below 93 and the straighten moves below ₹4.8 Cr.
2

One re-priced pass: search → spot → correct → search

gain: closes the 10 % search/true gap, better route rankingeffort: 2 dayspipeline.run_solve · solver.SolverParams

After the first spotting, compute per-component correction factors from the built route: tower price by type including extensions, strip per class, site cost by soil. Scale the search prices by those factors, drop the steering discounts to half, and search once more inside a 1 km corridor of the first route. Keep whichever spotted route is cheaper. Two passes are enough; the gain comes from the search ranking the alternatives at the price they will actually cost.

3

Refine where it matters

gain: 2–3 min per routeeffort: half a daypipeline.refine_pass · solver.refine

The 25 m pass re-searched the entire 113 km corridor and kept 318 of 318 towers. Restrict its column window to 1 km either side of each angle point and each controlled crossing of the 50 m solution; straight runs between them keep the 50 m answer. The per-column row window already exists, so this is a column mask on top of it.

4

Spot crossings in parallel

gain: 4–6 min per routeeffort: 1–2 daysspotting._refine_crossings · _crossing_side_fix

The crossing refinement took seven of the fifteen spotting minutes, one crossing at a time, each a search over candidate tower pairs with exact Shapely checks. Crossings more than two spans apart are independent. Run them in a process pool over a pickled slice of the checker, merge the results, then run the chain-wide checks once. The straighten, merge and vertex passes can also be batched by independent runs.

5

Clearance height per edge, priced before the search

gain: ₹2–5 Cr less in extensions, fewer ridge towers, height becomes a search priceeffort: 1 day for the steps, 3–4 days for the per-edge heightsolver._edge_block clearance · SolverParams.extension_m

The search knows one extension of 18 m and prices a span either clear or +18 m on one tower. The spotting has 3, 6, 18, 33 and 45 m steps and chooses the cheapest that clears. First step: carry the shortfall from the search's clearance sample and price the nearest step, so a 4 m shortfall costs ₹0.8 L, not ₹4.5 L, and the search stops detouring round small rises. Second step, which no published method has done jointly with routing either: precompute on the GPU, per lattice edge, the cheapest tower-height pair that clears the DEM profile with the sag template, and fold that price into the edge. Height then enters the search as a price and the leg programme resolves it exactly.

10

One backward pass: cap gap closure and alternatives for free

gain: replaces 6 cap passes and 3 penalty re-runs with one 50 s pass; cheapest route under the cap; alternatives with a quality boundeffort: 3–4 dayssolver.solve (reverse sweep) · pipeline forest_cap · routes 2–4

Run the same column sweep once from B back to A. Every state then has a cost-from-A and a cost-to-B, and their sum is the price of the best route through that state. Two things fall out. For the forest cap: enumerate routes whose sum is within the current Lagrangian gap of the best, and keep the cheapest with at most 5 ha. That is the Carlyle–Royset–Wood gap closure, and it turns the bisection's "a feasible route" into "the cheapest feasible route" with two forward passes and one backward. For alternatives: rank states by their sum, filter by how much of route 1 they share and by stretch, and string-pull the survivors. That is the via-node method of Abraham, Delling, Goldberg and Werneck, and it replaces the lateral, relaxed and overlap re-runs with alternatives that carry a provable bound.

11

Coarse pass first on wide bands

gain: search time ÷ 5 or better on a 50 km band, no measured cost loss above 5 %effort: 1–2 dayspipeline.run_solve · solver.solve corridor

Edge count grows with the fourth power of resolution. A 100 m pass over the full band, then the 50 m search confined to a 2 km corridor of its answer, is what the ETH work measured at under 13 % of the run time for under 5 % cost change. The corridor mechanism already exists for the 25 m pass.

12

Leg-graph vertex siting with the exact tower programme as edge cost

gain: search and spotted price coincide by construction; replaces string-pulling, merges and vertex descenteffort: 2–3 weeksnew module between solver and spotting

Inside the 50 m corridor, candidate angle-point sites form a graph. An edge is a straight leg of any length up to a few kilometres, priced by running the existing 1-D tower programme along it, batched on the GPU for thousands of legs at once. A node pays the exact angle tower for its deflection. A shortest path on the edge-based graph then picks the angle points and every tower between them in one exact pass. This is the 2019 Santos–de Queiroz three-stage formulation that beat a built 525 kV design by 14 %. Keep the single-tower polish afterwards; drop the greedy moves.

6

Forest hectares as a second resource

gain: 5 min per capped route; the cheapest route under the cap instead of a feasible oneeffort: 4–6 dayssolver.solve · pipeline forest_cap

Add a forest-area bucket to the state: six buckets of 1 ha for a 5 ha cap. A span adds its forest metres × corridor width to the bucket; states over the cap are dropped. Memory grows by the bucket count on the ring buffer only, since the DP keeps a few columns at a time. One pass replaces six, and the answer is the true constrained optimum. Keep the multiplier loop as the fallback when the bucket count would blow memory on a very wide band.

7

Cache the compiled rule set

gain: 2.5 min per solve, more on re-solveseffort: 1–2 dayscompile.compile_rules · rastercache

Key the whole compiled output (masks, strip, site cost, crossing rasters) on a hash of the resolved rules, the cost sheet, the layer version ids and the frame. A re-solve with a rule change still recompiles, but the four routes, the cap passes and the re-priced pass share one compile, and a re-solve with only a cost change skips it.

8

Close the checker-only gaps

gain: violations the client's reviewer would otherwise findeffort: 1–2 daysspotting.Checker · scripts/independent_check.py

The independent checker on solve 87 flagged a 66 kV crossing at 68.8° where the span crosses two features and the engine measured only one, and a canal parallel run at 19 m inside the 45 m buffer. Measure the crossing angle at every feature a span crosses, run the parallel check on canals whether or not the span also crosses one, and make the checker part of the test suite so each spot is verified before export.

9

A local lattice at each power-line crossing

gain: more crossings at the null point and 90°, fewer relaxed crossings than 50 of 55effort: 3–4 daysspotting._refine_crossings

Replace the sliding repair with a small exact search: a 10 m lattice of candidate positions for the two crossing towers inside the 87–123 m window, the angle window and the null-point band, scored with the true tower prices and the neighbours' resulting deflections. It is a few thousand candidate pairs per crossing, cheap in NumPy, and it finds the legal layout the greedy slide reported missing.

What the literature says

A research pass on 15 Oct 2026 over transmission-line routing, tower spotting, constrained and alternative shortest paths. Papers marked read were opened in full; the rest from abstracts.

The lattice search is the right design

Wiedemann and Adjiashvili (2021, arXiv 2101.03388, ETH Zurich with Gilytics, open source as lion-sp; read) search directly on a graph of candidate pylon sites with every span vector in an annulus and an angle cost between consecutive spans, restricted to a DAG along A–B. On their instances the direct pylon search beat route-then-spot by 10–20 % and angle cost fell by more than half. Our engine already does this. Their multi-scale result, under 13 % of run time for under 5 % cost change, is fix 11. Pivtoraiko, Knepper and Kelly (2009, J. Field Robotics; abstract) give the property our lattice lacks between 0° and 7.1°: the heading set must be closed under "continue straight", which fix 1 restores.

Unifying search price and built price

Santos, de Lima and de Queiroz (2019, Electric Power Systems Research 176; read) use three stages: a raster guideline, vertex siting on a graph of candidate angle points whose edges are straight legs priced exactly, and a dynamic-programme tower spotting over chainage and height with mandatory towers at angle points and crossings. On the 525 kV Machadinho–Campos Novos line it was 14 % cheaper than the built design, in about five minutes of single-threaded C. Medeiros et al. (2021, Int. Trans. Electr. Energy Syst.; abstract) is the reference formulation for the spotting programme with land use, slope, geotechnics and tension ratings, 3.8 % saving on 39 km. These are fix 12 and the benchmark that says our 15 minutes of Python spotting has headroom.

The forest cap and the alternatives share one trick

Carlyle, Royset and Wood (2008, Networks 52; read) solve the constrained shortest path by Lagrangian relaxation, then close the duality gap by enumerating near-shortest paths from forward and backward distances. On a 450 × 300 grid they reached a 0.5 % gap in 0.3 s. Dumitrescu and Boland (2003, Networks 42; abstract) is the exact label-setting alternative, which on our DAG is the forest bucket in the state, fix 6. Abraham, Delling, Goldberg and Werneck (2010, arXiv 1002.4330; read) define an admissible alternative as sharing at most 80 % of the optimum with bounded stretch and local optimality, found through via-nodes scored by forward plus backward distance. Kobitzsch, Radermacher and Schieferdecker (2013, ATMOS; read) give the penalty settings if re-runs are kept: a 4 % additive factor on the shared edges plus a rejoin penalty. Together these are fix 10.

Backward detours and height

Monteiro, Ramírez-Rosado and Miranda (2005, IEEE Trans. Power Delivery 20; read) iterate a label-correcting search on a raster with direction-change costs to a fixed point, which allows non-monotone routes, and derive economic corridors. Wiedemann restricts to a DAG and reports no loss, which suggests back-tracking is rare and a coarse detector is enough. No published method searches route and tower height jointly; Wiedemann lists it as open. The pieces exist: the chainage × height spotting programme (Mitra 1968, Medeiros 2021) and, recently, profile-peak candidate sites with a metaheuristic for heights (Li et al. 2026, IET GTD; abstract). Fix 5 takes the practical route of pricing height into the edge.

Checked and set aside

Field D*, Theta* and ANYA give continuous headings on cost rasters but apply to raster corridors, not to a span lattice, and ANYA assumes uniform cost. Multicriteria raster least-cost-path papers (Bagli, Geneletti and Orsi 2011; Biasotto et al. 2022) stop at the corridor. Gilytics Pathfinder has no methodology paper beyond the ETH work. Metaheuristics (GA, annealing, RRT*) give no guarantee and are slower than the exact search we already have.
