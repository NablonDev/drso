Beeline Solver Explained

How the route is found, what the ₹ it minimises is made of, and what three of the known gaps mean. Written for a reader who knows transmission lines, not search algorithms.

Open the animated simulator to watch the same method run on a sample corridor, stage by stage.

What kind of algorithm is running

A cheapest-path search on a grid, solved exactly by dynamic programming, on the GPU. The band between A and B is covered with candidate tower positions every 50 m. Every allowed span between two positions is a move with a price. The solver works through the grid once, from A towards B, keeping for every position the cheapest way to reach it, and then reads the cheapest chain back from B. It is the same family as the shortest-path routines in a satnav (a Viterbi / DAG shortest path), not a genetic algorithm, not simulated annealing, not an LLM. For the grid it is given, the answer is provably the cheapest; the tests check it against a brute-force search.

Two things make it feel smart. First, every rule is turned into geometry before the search starts, so the search never has to "know" the rules, only the prices and the forbidden cells. Second, the grid answer is only a draft: a second, finer search and a tower-by-tower polish bring it down to real tower positions and a real price.

Step by step

Six pictures. The pipeline in code: compile → solve → refine → forest cap → spot → log.

1

Straighten the map

The map is rotated so the straight line from A to B runs left to right. Everything inside the band is drawn on a 20 m grid in that frame. "Forward" now simply means "to the right", which is what lets the search be exact and fast.

The cost of this: the route can wander sideways as much as it likes, but it can never go backwards along the bee-line. A U-shaped detour round a block wider than the band cannot be found.

2

Turn every rule into a picture

Each of the 52 rules is compiled into one of a few things on that grid: cells where no tower may stand, cells no wire may pass over, cells where only a straight-line tower may stand, a price per m² of corridor (land compensation, forest diversion), a foundation multiplier (soil), and for crossable features the direction of the thing being crossed.

Buffers come from distance transforms, accurate to about one cell. The tower spotting later re-checks every tower against the exact vector shapes, so a buffer that is off by a few metres on the grid is corrected at the end.

3

Lay down the candidate positions

Candidate tower sites sit every 50 m along and across the band. From any site, the next tower can be any site that is at least 100 m and at most the design span away (400 m at 765 kV), ahead rather than behind, and not too far off to the side. That fan of allowed moves is the same at every site, which is what lets the GPU evaluate millions of them at once.

A move is thrown away if the span would pass over a conductor-forbidden cell, if the target site is tower-forbidden, or if it crosses a river, road, railway or line at an angle or length the crossing rule does not allow.

4

Price every move

Each move from one tower to the next carries three prices, all in rupees, all taken from the Costs screen. This is the quantity the search minimises: the sum of these three over the whole chain from gantry A to gantry B.

S

Standing here

What this spot adds regardless of tower type: the foundation extra for the soil (black cotton ×1.55, rock ×1.2, marsh and piles ×1.85) and the tower-base land compensation. Infinite if the spot is forbidden.

T

The bend

The tower's own price, chosen by how sharply the line turns here: A for a straight run, B1 to 15°, C1 to 30°, D45, D60. At 765 kV that is ₹0.48 Cr for an A and ₹1.11 Cr for a D60. A crossing that forces a heavier type pays the heavier price. Over 60° is not allowed.

E

The span

Length × line cost per metre (₹2.35 Cr/km at 765 kV) plus the right-of-way strip under it: length × corridor width × the price per m² of the ground it crosses, averaged along the span. Agricultural land pays 30 % of land value; reserve forest pays NPV, afforestation, plans and land.

On top of these the search adds steering prices that are later removed again: a 20 % discount near existing lines, a surcharge on hill ground, a graded penalty for crossing a power line away from its null point, and on routes 2 and 4 a penalty for sideways offset or for copying route 1. They move the route; they never appear in the reported ₹.

5

Sweep once, left to right

The solver takes the columns in order. For each site in a column it asks: of all the moves that could land here, which one gives the lowest total so far? It stores that number and remembers where it came from. The state it keeps is not just the site but the site plus the direction it arrived from, because the price of the next bend depends on the incoming direction. The whole column is computed at once on the GPU.

When the sweep reaches B, the cheapest total is known and the chain is read back through the remembered "came from" links. There is no guessing and no random restarts. Different routes come from running the same sweep again with a different steering price, not from a different method.

6

Make it real, then price it honestly

The grid chain is a draft at 50 m resolution. A second sweep on a 25 m lattice, confined to 300 m either side of the draft, tightens it. If a strict route carries more than 5 ha of reserve forest, the sweep repeats with forest priced up until it does not.

Then the tower spotting takes over: runs of grid towers become straight legs between angle points, every tower is checked against the exact vector buffers and nudged if needed, towers along each leg are placed by a 10 m cost programme, crossing towers are slid into their windows, section towers added, ground clearance checked on the DEM, and bends merged or moved where that is cheaper. The ₹ you see is recomputed from these final towers with the true prices, never the search price.

Three of the known gaps, explained

now enforced · README line is stale

"n±1 deviation" at power-line crossings

Call the span that passes over the existing line "span n". The towers at its two ends are the crossing towers, which the AESL sheet calls n and n+1 (or "n±1" seen from the crossing point). The rule says those two towers should not be sharp angle points: the line should arrive at the crossing straight and leave it straight, so the crossing towers can be the standard type and the conductor sits squarely over the other line.

Until 13 October the engine only wrote a warning when one of them turned hard. Since then the cap is a real rule: each crossing tower may turn at most 45° (the "Max deviation at the two crossing towers" parameter on every crossing rule). The search uses a reduced bend table on crossing spans, the spotting records a violation named crossing_deviation if a tower still exceeds it, and a repair step shares the turn with a neighbour moved up to 200 m. The README sentence calling it a warning predates this change.

CRZ measured from the sea polygon, not the HTL

Coastal Regulation Zone rules are defined from the High Tide Line, the legal line the coastal authority draws, and split the land behind it into zones I to IV with different restrictions. The delivered CRZ file so far contains only the sea and Gulf water polygon. The HTL line and the zone polygons have not been delivered.

So the engine takes the edge of the water polygon as a stand-in for the HTL and keeps towers and conductor the corridor half-width back from it (33.5 m at 765 kV), crossable only on the relaxed route with 12 months' approval. The layer already has class slots for the zones, so they switch on when the data arrives. There is also no money attached: the cost sheet of 7 October removed the environment / CRZ clearance item, so entering the zone adds nothing to the ₹.

data pending

Wetland: the 50 m high-flood-level setback

The AESL rule table says wetland is routable, but no foundation may be placed within 50 m of the 10-year mean High Flood Level, the water level a once-in-ten-years flood reaches. That line is a hydrology product: it needs flood records or a flood model per wetland, and no such layer has been delivered for Gujarat.

Without it the engine cannot draw the setback. What it does instead: towers on wetland are allowed, every one of them is costed on a pile foundation (×1.85 the normal foundation), and the wetland polygon itself carries no buffer. Where an HFL layer arrives, the rule needs one new buffer parameter and no engine change.
