Three choices per layer. Auto-fetch lets the system get it — from a cached dataset or by running a vision model over the imagery. Replace uses your file instead and skips the model, which is faster and usually more accurate when you hold the authoritative data. Add more runs the auto-fetch and merges your file on top; where the two disagree, yours wins.

A rule is a set of parameters, not one number. The railway rule alone carries six — tower setback, the structure-height allowance that produces it, the crossing angle range, the span cap, the percentage-of-design-span alternative, and the tower type. The list shows all of them at their current values; Change opens them all for editing side by side with their defaults.
Land classes are go or no-go, nothing in between. There are no avoidance sliders and no weights on this screen. How strongly the engine prefers one class over another is an internal model parameter, not a business decision — the drafter sets what the route may and may not enter, and the engine handles the rest.

How this page is written without an LLM

Every sentence comes from a template keyed to a typed event in the decision log the Python engine emits. Around twenty-five templates cover the vocabulary. Nothing is generated at read time — the log is the deliverable, the page is only its renderer.

TURN_TO_AVOID → "At km {chainage}, the route turns {angle}° {dir} at tower {tower} ({type}) to clear {feature} by {clearance} m." [+ {source}, {confidence}] CROSSING → "Crosses {feature} at {angle}° on a {span} m span between two Type {type} towers." LAYER_EMPTY → "{rule} did not fire — the {layer} extraction found nothing in the band." LOW_CONF → "{decision} rests on {layer} at {confidence} confidence. Verify on site."
Provenance is part of the event, not a footnote. Because the drafter chose per layer where the data came from, every sentence has to carry whether it was auto-fetched, supplied, or merged — and how sure the model was.
